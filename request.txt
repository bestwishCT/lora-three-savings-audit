010: ABSTRACT
011: An important paradigm of natural language processing consists of large-scale pre-
012: training on general domain data and adaptation to particular tasks or domains. As
013: we pre-train larger models, full fine-tuning, which retrains all model parameters,
014: becomes less feasible. Using GPT-3 175B as an example – deploying indepen-
015: dent instances of fine-tuned models, each with 175B parameters, is prohibitively
016: expensive. We propose Low-Rank Adaptation, or LoRA, which freezes the pre-
017: trained model weights and injects trainable rank decomposition matrices into each
018: layer of the Transformer architecture, greatly reducing the number of trainable pa-
019: rameters for downstream tasks. Compared to GPT-3 175B fine-tuned with Adam,
020: LoRA can reduce the number of trainable parameters by 10,000 times and the
021: GPU memory requirement by 3 times. LoRA performs on-par or better than fine-
022: tuning in model quality on RoBERTa, DeBERTa, GPT-2, and GPT-3, despite hav-
023: ing fewer trainable parameters, a higher training throughput, and, unlike adapters,
024: no additional inference latency . We also provide an empirical investigation into
025: rank-deficiency in language model adaptation, which sheds light on the efficacy of
026: LoRA. We release a package that facilitates the integration of LoRA with PyTorch
027: models and provide our implementations and model checkpoints for RoBERTa,
028: DeBERTa, and GPT-2 athttps://github.com/microsoft/LoRA.

012: 4 O UR METHOD
013: We describe the simple design of LoRA and its practical benefits. The principles outlined here apply
014: to any dense layers in deep learning models, though we only focus on certain weights in Transformer
015: language models in our experiments as the motivating use case.
016: 4.1 L OW-R ANK -PARAMETRIZED UPDATE MATRICES
017: A neural network contains many dense layers which perform matrix multiplication. The weight
018: matrices in these layers typically have full-rank. When adapting to a specific task, Aghajanyan et al.
019: (2020) shows that the pre-trained language models have a low “instrisic dimension” and can still
020: learn efficiently despite a random projection to a smaller subspace. Inspired by this, we hypothe-
021: size the updates to the weights also have a low “intrinsic rank” during adaptation. For a pre-trained
022: weight matrix W0∈ Rd×k, we constrain its update by representing the latter with a low-rank de-
023: compositionW0 + ∆W =W0 +BA, whereB∈ Rd×r,A∈ Rr×k, and the rank r≪ min(d,k ).
024: During training,W0 is frozen and does not receive gradient updates, whileA andB contain trainable
025: parameters. Note both W0 and ∆W =BA are multiplied with the same input, and their respective
026: output vectors are summed coordinate-wise. Forh =W0x, our modified forward pass yields:
027: h =W0x + ∆Wx =W0x +BAx (3)
028: We illustrate our reparametrization in Figure 1. We use a random Gaussian initialization for A and
029: zero forB, so ∆W =BA is zero at the beginning of training. We then scale ∆Wx by αr , whereαis a constant inr. When optimizing with Adam, tuningα is roughly the same as tuning the learning
030: rate if we scale the initialization appropriately. As a result, we simply set α to the first r we try
031: and do not tune it. This scaling helps to reduce the need to retune hyperparameters when we vary
032: r (Yang & Hu, 2021).
033: A Generalization of Full Fine-tuning. A more general form of fine-tuning allows the training of
034: a subset of the pre-trained parameters. LoRA takes a step further and does not require the accumu-
035: lated gradient update to weight matrices to have full-rank during adaptation. This means that when
036: applying LoRA to all weight matrices and training all biases 2, we roughly recover the expressive-
037: ness of full fine-tuning by setting the LoRA rankr to the rank of the pre-trained weight matrices. In
038: other words, as we increase the number of trainable parameters 3, training LoRA roughly converges
039: to training the original model, while adapter-based methods converges to an MLP and prefix-based
040: methods to a model that cannot take long input sequences.
041: No Additional Inference Latency. When deployed in production, we can explicitly compute and
042: store W = W0 +BA and perform inference as usual. Note that both W0 and BA are in Rd×k.
043: When we need to switch to another downstream task, we can recover W0 by subtracting BA and
044: then adding a different B′A′, a quick operation with very little memory overhead. Critically, this

003: 4.2 A PPLYING LORA TO TRANSFORMER
004: In principle, we can apply LoRA to any subset of weight matrices in a neural network to reduce the
005: number of trainable parameters. In the Transformer architecture, there are four weight matrices in
006: the self-attention module (Wq,Wk,Wv,Wo) and two in the MLP module. We treatWq (orWk,Wv)
007: as a single matrix of dimensiondmodel×dmodel, even though the output dimension is usually sliced
008: into attention heads. We limit our study to only adapting the attention weights for downstream
009: tasks and freeze the MLP modules (so they are not trained in downstream tasks) both for simplicity
010: and parameter-efficiency.We further study the effect on adapting different types of attention weight
011: matrices in a Transformer in Section 7.1. We leave the empirical investigation of adapting the MLP
012: layers, LayerNorm layers, and biases to a future work.
013: Practical Benefits and Limitations. The most significant benefit comes from the reduction in
014: memory and storage usage. For a large Transformer trained with Adam, we reduce that VRAM
015: usage by up to 2/3 if r≪ dmodel as we do not need to store the optimizer states for the frozen
016: parameters. On GPT-3 175B, we reduce the VRAM consumption during training from 1.2TB to
017: 350GB. Withr = 4 and only the query and value projection matrices being adapted, the checkpoint
018: size is reduced by roughly 10,000× (from 350GB to 35MB) 4. This allows us to train with signifi-
019: cantly fewer GPUs and avoid I/O bottlenecks. Another benefit is that we can switch between tasks
020: while deployed at a much lower cost by only swapping the LoRA weights as opposed to all the
021: parameters. This allows for the creation of many customized models that can be swapped in and out
022: on the fly on machines that store the pre-trained weights in VRAM. We also observe a 25% speedup
023: during training on GPT-3 175B compared to full fine-tuning 5 as we do not need to calculate the
024: gradient for the vast majority of the parameters.
025: LoRA also has its limitations. For example, it is not straightforward to batch inputs to different tasks
026: with differentA andB in a single forward pass, if one chooses to absorbA andB intoW to eliminate
027: additional inference latency. Though it is possible to not merge the weights and dynamically choose
028: the LoRA modules to use for samples in a batch for scenarios where latency is not critical.
029: 5 E MPIRICAL EXPERIMENTS
030: We evaluate the downstream task performance of LoRA on RoBERTa (Liu et al., 2019), De-
031: BERTa (He et al., 2021), and GPT-2 (Radford et al., b), before scaling up to GPT-3 175B (Brown
032: et al., 2020). Our experiments cover a wide range of tasks, from natural language understanding
033: (NLU) to generation (NLG). Specifically, we evaluate on the GLUE (Wang et al., 2019) benchmark
034: for RoBERTa and DeBERTa. We follow the setup of Li & Liang (2021) on GPT-2 for a direct com-
035: parison and add WikiSQL (Zhong et al., 2017) (NL to SQL queries) and SAMSum (Gliwa et al.,
036: 2019) (conversation summarization) for large-scale experiments on GPT-3. See Appendix C for
037: more details on the datasets we use. We use NVIDIA Tesla V100 for all experiments.
